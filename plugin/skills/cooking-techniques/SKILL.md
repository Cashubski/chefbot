# Techniques and cuisines

## Teaching a technique

1. Say what the technique achieves and the one principle behind it (browning needs a dry surface and high heat; emulsions need slow addition and agitation; braises need low heat and time for collagen).
2. Give the steps as a short numbered method with the cues that tell the user it is working, from [references/techniques.md](references/techniques.md).
3. Name the two most common mistakes and how to recognise them.
4. Suggest one simple dish to practise it on, using what the user has (fridge-to-meal skill).
5. Offer a demonstration video only if a search tool is available in this conversation and finds a reputable one; never invent a link.

## Exploring a cuisine

1. Ask or infer what the user already likes and what equipment and shops they have. Many cuisines are feasible with a few pantry items; say which.
2. Introduce the cuisine through its **pantry** (the five to eight ingredients that define it), its **techniques**, and **three dishes** ordered from easiest to most involved.
3. Mark clearly what is **traditional** and what is an **adaptation** for the user's kitchen or ingredients. Do not present an adaptation as authentic.
4. Respect the cuisine's own names and spellings; give a plain-language description beside them.
5. Suggest the pairing and the order of a meal if the user wants to cook a full spread (drink-pairings and cook-along skills).

## Rules

- Explain the science only to the depth that changes what the user does.
- Cuisines are described with respect and specificity: regions, not continents; dishes, not stereotypes.
- Cultural and religious food rules (halal, kosher, fasting) are followed exactly when the user raises them (dietary-adaptation skill).

## Output

For a technique: principle, method, cues, mistakes, a practice dish. For a cuisine: pantry, techniques, three dishes with difficulty and time, traditional versus adapted notes.
